QUESTION 1 - RESEARCH REPORT ON PROJECT MANAGEMENT

1. WHAT IS A PROJECT AND WHAT IS PROJECT MANAGEMENT

A project is a temporary effort undertaken to create a unique product,
service, or result. "Temporary" means it has a defined start and end;
"unique" means the outcome differs from routine, repetitive operations.
A project ends when its objectives are met, when it becomes clear the
objectives cannot be met, or when the need for it disappears.

Project management is the application of knowledge, skills, tools, and
techniques to project activities in order to meet defined objectives
within agreed constraints. A project manager plans the work, assembles
and coordinates the team, tracks progress against a baseline, manages
risk, and communicates status to stakeholders. Project management is
not the same as product management: a project has an end date and a
fixed set of deliverables, while a product is managed continuously for
as long as it exists in the market.

Every project operates inside constraints. The most common way to
describe them is the scope-time-cost triangle, with quality placed at
the centre because quality is affected by all three sides at once.


2. THE SCOPE-TIME-COST TRIANGLE

Scope is what the project must deliver. Time is the schedule available.
Cost is the budget and the people-hours it can consume. These three are
interdependent: changing one, without a matching change elsewhere, is
absorbed by quality.

  Corner squeezed   Typical consequence if the other two stay fixed
  ----------------  ------------------------------------------------
  Scope reduced      Fewer features ship; quality per feature can
                      stay high because effort concentrates on less.
  Scope increased     Either time or cost must increase too, or
                      quality drops because the same effort now
                      covers more ground.
  Time reduced        Corners are cut under pressure: less testing,
                      less review, more technical debt. Quality
                      suffers first because it is the least visible
                      corner to a sponsor watching the deadline.
  Time increased      More room for testing and iteration, but the
                      project costs more in people-hours and may
                      miss its market window.
  Cost reduced        Fewer or more junior people; scope or time
                      must shrink to compensate, or output quality
                      drops.
  Cost increased      More people or better people, useful for
                      time but not infinitely: adding people to a
                      late project can slow it further because of
                      coordination overhead.

The lesson project managers draw from this triangle is that scope,
time, and cost cannot all be fixed simultaneously without a quality
cost. A sponsor who fixes all three has, in effect, already decided
that quality is the variable that will absorb the difference.


3. WATERFALL VERSUS AGILE

Waterfall plans the entire project up front and executes it in
sequential phases. Agile plans a small increment, builds it, gets
feedback, and re-plans. The two differ on several practical dimensions.

Neither approach is universally better. Waterfall suits work where the
requirements are genuinely stable and the cost of late change is high
(construction, regulated hardware). Agile suits work where the
requirements are expected to change as the team and the customer learn
more, which describes most software products, including FoodFast in
Question 2.


4. SCRUM

Scrum is a framework for applying Agile ideas with a fixed structure of
roles, artefacts, and events.

Roles (3):
  - Product Owner: owns the backlog, decides what gets built and in
    what order, represents the customer's interests.
  - Scrum Master: protects the process, removes blockers, coaches the
    team; not a project manager who assigns tasks.
  - Development Team: cross-functional, self-organising group that
    builds the increment.

Artefacts (3):
  - Product Backlog: the ordered list of everything that might be
    built, owned and prioritised by the Product Owner.
  - Sprint Backlog: the subset of backlog items the team commits to
    for the current sprint, plus the plan to deliver them.
  - Increment: the sum of all completed backlog items, in a state that
    meets the Definition of Done, potentially shippable.

Events (4), with time-boxes for a 2-week sprint:
  - Sprint Planning: up to 4 hours. The team selects backlog items and
    plans how to deliver them.
  - Daily Scrum: 15 minutes, every working day. The team synchronises
    and surfaces blockers.
  - Sprint Review: up to 2 hours. The team demonstrates the increment
    to stakeholders and gathers feedback.
  - Sprint Retrospective: up to 1.5 hours. The team inspects its own
    process and agrees concrete improvements for the next sprint.

(Time-boxes scale with sprint length; the figures above are the
standard maximums for a 2-week sprint, roughly half the maximums used
for a 4-week sprint.)


5. KANBAN

Kanban is a method for visualising and managing flow, built on three
core principles:

  1. Visualise the workflow: represent every stage of work as a column
     on a board so bottlenecks are visible.
  2. Limit work in progress (WIP): cap the number of items allowed in
     each active column at one time.
  3. Manage and measure flow: track how long items take to move across
     the board (lead time and cycle time) and act on the data.

The WIP limit is the principle most often ignored in practice, usually
because it feels uncomfortable to stop starting new work. Without it,
a Kanban board is just a to-do list split into columns: people keep
pulling new items into "In Progress" while old items stall, and the
board stops revealing anything about where work is actually stuck. The
WIP limit is what forces a team to finish work before starting more,
which is the entire point of a flow-based method.


6. REQUIREMENTS ENGINEERING

A requirement is a statement of what a system must do or a quality it
must have. Requirements split into two kinds:

  - Functional requirements describe behaviour: what the system does
    in response to inputs. Example: "the system shall let a customer
    cancel an order within 5 minutes of placing it."
  - Non-functional requirements describe qualities of the system:
    performance, security, availability, usability, scalability.
    Example: "the search API shall respond with p95 latency under
    500 ms under 200 concurrent users."

A good requirement is:
  - Unambiguous: only one reasonable interpretation.
  - Verifiable: someone can test whether it was met.
  - Necessary: traceable to a real business or user need.
  - Feasible: achievable within the project's constraints.
  - Prioritised: not everything can be "must have."
  - Consistent: does not contradict another requirement.

The most common failure is writing a requirement that sounds precise
but cannot be tested. "The app must be fast" is not a requirement,
because nobody can say, after building it, whether it passed or
failed. "The search API responds with p95 under 500 ms" is a
requirement, because it is a number a test can check against.


7. USER STORIES

A user story is a short, informal description of a feature written
from the perspective of the person who wants it, using the template:

  As a [role], I want to [action] so that [benefit].

Example: "As a customer, I want to save my delivery address so that I
do not retype it on every order."

A well-formed story satisfies the INVEST criteria:
  - Independent: can be built and delivered without depending on
    another unfinished story.
  - Negotiable: a starting point for a conversation, not a fixed
    contract.
  - Valuable: delivers value to a user or the business.
  - Estimable: the team can size it with reasonable confidence.
  - Small: fits inside a single sprint.
  - Testable: has clear acceptance criteria.
